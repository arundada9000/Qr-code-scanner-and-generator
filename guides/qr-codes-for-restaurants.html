QR Codes for Restaurants

In a few years QR codes went from a novelty on a bar napkin to standard equipment in hospitality. A well-placed code lets a customer open your menu in seconds, order from their seat, and even pay — without a printed menu being touched, reprinted, or restocked. This guide covers the practical side: what restaurants actually use codes for, how to set them up without losing revenue to friction, and the mistakes that quietly hurt your customers' experience.

The three jobs a QR code can do

1. Contactless menus (the classic)

A code on each table opens your menu on the customer's phone. The benefits are obvious: menu updates are instant and free (no reprints when a dish sells out), and there's one less surface to clean. The code should be printed large enough to scan from a seated position — a credit-card-sized sticker on a stand works well — and it should always open a version of the menu that is easy to read on a phone, not a PDF designed for print.

2. Table ordering and pre-payment

Full table-ordering systems let customers browse, order, and pay without a server in the loop. These usually integrate with a POS and are worth it for fast-casual venues and busy weekends. A lighter option is ordering a first round of drinks via code, or letting customers request the bill and pay by card — a genuinely popular use that speeds up turnover at the end of service.

3. Marketing and loyalty

Menus are finite, but a QR code's destination doesn't have to be. Point a code at your Google Maps review page to make leaving a review a two-tap action. Point another at your social profile, your newsletter sign-up, or a loyalty card saved to the phone's wallet. Small touches like "Scan to review" on the receipt card measurably increase review volume because they remove all effort.

One table, many codes — or one code, many pages

Keep the tabletop simple. If you want the menu, ordering, and reviews to all be available, resist covering the table with five different codes. Instead:

  • Put the menu code on a permanent table stand.
  • Put a review/loyalty code on the bill card, the receipt, or at the exit.
  • Keep table tents to one clear message each. A customer glancing at a table stand should know instantly what scanning does.

If you want everything behind a single code, the code must point to a landing page with clear options — "View menu", "Order", "Leave a review". That's more work to build and maintain than separate codes, but it keeps the table clean.

Design and placement rules that matter

  • Size for distance. A table stand is scanned from about 30–60 cm away, so a code around 3 cm square is comfortable. A code on a window, door, or bar top is scanned from further — go bigger.
  • Dark on light, always. Black on white scans most reliably under the yellow light of a dining room. Glossy laminated surfaces can glare — matte is safer.
  • Give it a border. Keep a clear margin around the code. Don't let the words "SCAN ME" crowd the edge of the pattern.
  • Test in the room, at night. A code that works in daylight can fail under dim bar lighting. Test it from an actual table, with a phone, at the hour it will be used.

The silent revenue killer: friction

A QR menu only saves you money if customers actually use it. The fastest way to lose that advantage is making scanning feel like work:

  • No Wi-Fi needed. The menu page should load quickly on mobile data — don't assume everyone joins your Wi-Fi first.
  • Mobile-first page. If the menu is hard to zoom, flick through, or read on a phone, many customers will just ask for a paper menu.
  • Plain URL, not a forced app. Never make a customer install an app to see a menu. This is the single most-cited reason QR menus get abandoned.
  • Keep a printed backup. Older customers, poor phone batteries, and broken data connections still happen. Keep a few printed menus at the counter.

A practical setup with free tools

You don't need a paid platform to get the basics right. Put a copy of your menu on a Google Drive or any cheap hosting, and make sure the "share" link opens directly in a browser with the file viewable by anyone with the link. Then:

  1. Open the SajiloQR generator.
  2. Paste the menu URL and set the type to URL / Website.
  3. Download the PNG (the quiet zone is already included).
  4. Print it at the right size, or drop it into your table-stand design.
  5. Scan it with your own phone before it goes on the table — then test again from the actual table.

When the menu changes, update the file at the same link. The code doesn't change, which is the whole point — one code, living menu.

Security: your menu code is your brand

Anyone can print a code that looks official and point it somewhere malicious. Check the printed codes at your tables occasionally, especially if the venue is busy with lots of foot traffic. If you use a single URL, keep it short, memorable, and owned by you — so a sticker with a "helpful" replacement code is easy to spot. Customers scanning your menu should land on your menu, nothing else.
